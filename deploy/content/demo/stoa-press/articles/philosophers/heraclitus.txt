Heraclitus

Heraclitus of Ephesus (c. 535 – 475 BCE) is among the most enigmatic of the
early Greek thinkers, remembered in antiquity as "the obscure" for the
deliberate difficulty of his pronouncements. A citizen of the prosperous
Ionian city of Ephesus, he is said to have renounced a hereditary office and
withdrawn from public life, contemptuous of the crowd and of the eminent men
his contemporaries admired. His book survives only in fragments — terse,
oracular sayings whose compression invites endless interpretation.

The thought most firmly associated with him is that of universal flux.
"Everything flows," later writers summarized his teaching, and he is reported
to have said that one cannot step twice into the same river, for fresh waters
are ever flowing in. Reality, for Heraclitus, is not a collection of stable
things but a ceaseless process of change. Fire, the most mobile and
transformative of the elements, serves as his emblem of this world-process,
"kindling in measures and going out in measures."

Yet beneath the flux Heraclitus discerned an order, which he called the
Logos — a rational principle or measure that governs all change and that most
people, though they encounter it daily, fail to comprehend. The world is not
chaos but a hidden harmony, and "a hidden harmony is stronger than an obvious
one." Wisdom consists in grasping this underlying unity that binds the
apparent multiplicity of things.

Central to that unity is the coincidence of opposites. The road up and the
road down are one and the same; the way of writing is straight and crooked;
disease makes health sweet, hunger makes fullness pleasant. Opposites are not
merely connected but require one another, and the tension between them — war,
which Heraclitus called the father of all things — is the very condition of
existence. What appears as conflict is, rightly understood, the dynamic
balance that sustains the cosmos.

Heraclitus left no school, but his influence was profound and lasting. The
Stoics adopted his Logos and his fiery cosmology as cornerstones of their
physics, and his vision of a world in perpetual becoming has echoed through
the centuries down to the dialectical philosophies of the modern age. Few
thinkers have said so much in so few words.
